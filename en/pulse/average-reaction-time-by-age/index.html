Average reaction time by age

What a study of 1,469 adults measured, age group by age group, and why a few milliseconds are nothing to worry about.

Published 5 November 2026Updated 11 November 20266 min read
217.9ms

average of 18–24 year olds, lights out to key press

Short answer
Simple reaction time rises slowly with age: in a study of 1,469 adults, the average went from 217.9 ms in the 18–24 group to 239.1 ms in the 59–65 group, about 0.5 ms per year (Woods et al., 2015, Table 2). People of the same age usually differ by more than that: within each group the standard deviation was 19.5 to 28.1 ms, against about 21 ms between the youngest and the oldest group.

What makes you react faster or slower

Mostly age, sleep and the device you measure on; practice makes your average reliable.

+0.5 msper year

Age

Between 18 and 65, the average rises by about 0.5 ms per year (Woods et al., 2015). It is a slow change: from the youngest group to the oldest there are about 21 ms, roughly two hundredths of a second.

Fatigue and sleep

Sleep loss slows responses and makes lapses, responses that take longer than half a second, more frequent (Lim & Dinges, 2008).

1 → 5+tries

Practice

One try says little. Do several and keep the average; if you do many, rest between rounds, because even within one long session responses get a little slower (Woods et al., 2015).

≈18 ms in the study
up to 100 ms

Your device

Screens, mice and software can add up to 100 ms to the measured time (Woods et al., 2015). That is why an online test cannot be compared with the table to the millisecond: always compare on the same device.

Simple vs. choice reaction time

Simple

One signal, one response. Like the lights test above.

Choice

Several signals, each with its own response. Like Batak.

In a study of 7,130 adults, simple reaction time showed little slowing until around 50, whereas choice reaction time slowed throughout adulthood (Der & Deary, 2006).

Reaction time after 65

The table stops at 65 because that is where the study’s first experiment ends. A second experiment in a different laboratory tested 189 people aged 18 to 82: reaction times again increased with age, while the time needed to detect the stimulus stayed stable. The authors conclude that the age-related increase is due primarily to slowed motor output, that is, to starting the movement rather than seeing the signal (Woods et al., 2015). With several possible signals the picture is different: in a study of 7,130 adults, choice reaction time slowed throughout adulthood (Der & Deary, 2006). If something about your own reflexes worries you, an online test is no substitute for a health professional.

Men and women

Studies do not agree. In the large 18-to-65 sample of Woods and colleagues, simple reaction time was unaffected by sex (Woods et al., 2015). A UK survey of 7,130 adults found significant sex differences, most notably in how variable choice reaction times were (Der & Deary, 2006). A study of 120 first-year medical students aged 18 to 20, 60 men and 60 women, found faster times in men for both light and sound (Jain et al., 2015). The same study points to another factor, physical activity: only 20 of the 120 students exercised regularly, 16 men and 4 women, and in that small group the difference between men and women was not significant. No rule applies to you in particular: compare yourself with yourself.

Athletes and exercise

A 2025 systematic review of 35 studies compared athletes with non-athletes on tasks of memory, attention and decision-making. Most studies reported that athletes outperformed controls, both in accuracy and in reaction time; martial arts and boxing showed good reaction times but poor accuracy (Delgado et al., 2025). The student study above also found faster times in regularly exercising students than in sedentary ones (Jain et al., 2015), and action video game players responded 11% faster on choice tasks (Dye et al., 2009). These are comparisons between groups: on their own they cannot show whether training makes people faster or faster people tend to train, and they promise nothing to any individual.

Is my time normal?

This guide is educational: an online test cannot diagnose anything.

The averages in the table were measured in a lab on calibrated equipment; your browser and screen add their own delay, so compare yourself mainly with yourself. If you notice your reflexes have changed suddenly, talk to a health professional.

FAQ

At what age is reaction time fastest?

In the youngest group of the study, aged 18 to 24: 217.9 ms on average. From there the average rose in every age group up to 65 (Woods et al., 2015, Table 2).

Do video games improve reflexes?

On fast choice tasks, action video game players responded 11% faster than non-players, with the same accuracy. Some training studies indicate that playing can speed up responses (Dye et al., 2009).

What is the difference between simple and choice reaction time?

Simple reaction has one signal and one response, like our test. Choice reaction has several signals, each with its own response. In a study of 7,130 adults, simple reaction time showed little slowing until around 50, whereas choice reaction time slowed throughout adulthood (Der & Deary, 2006).
