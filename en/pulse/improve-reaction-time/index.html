How to improve reaction time

What the research says actually moves your reaction time, what barely does, and how to check your own progress with a test you can take right here.

Published 18 November 2026Updated 20 November 20267 min read

Short answer
You get faster mostly at the task you practise, and slower when short of sleep or tired. Practice carries over only partly to other tasks (Bavelier & Green, 2025). Age adds about 0.5 ms a year from 18 to 65; a slow device can add up to 100 ms (Woods et al., 2015).

What affects reaction time the most?

Sleep, fatigue and practice on the task move it the most; age changes it only slowly, and your device adds delay to what you measure.

Even in one long session, average times rose from 228.1 ms in the first block to 237.2 ms in the last (Woods et al., 2015). Time of day is a separate factor that researchers measure on its own (Blatter & Cajochen, 2007).

Your device

Screens, mice and software can add up to 100 ms to the measured time (Woods et al., 2015), so always compare on the same device.

Can you train reaction time?

Yes, but the gains mostly stay with the task you practise.

Practise the same task

Training on one task typically improves that task alone, with limited transfer to untrained tasks (Bavelier & Green, 2025). In a choice reaction study, transfer was smaller when the response changed from keypresses to joystick movements, though it did not disappear (Yamaguchi & Proctor, 2009).

Fast action games

Action video game players responded 11% faster than non-players on fast choice tasks, with the same accuracy (Dye et al., 2009).

In a six-week study of 12 to 16 year old field hockey players, a vision training program improved a trained choice reaction time task, but there was no significant gain in an untrained object-tracking task (Schwab & Memmert, 2012). Evidence on games is mixed too: in two experiments with five supervised sessions, action gamers, cognitive gamers and non-gamers showed similar practice effects on a simple perceptual task (van Ravenzwaaij et al., 2014). No app or game can promise a specific gain.

Because gains mostly stay with the trained task, it makes sense to practise something that looks like the task you care about: a lights test for simple reactions, a pad-tapping test for choices. Our reaction time test and choice reaction time test measure these two separately.

Does sleep affect reaction time?

Yes: sleep loss slows responses and makes lapses more frequent.

> 0.5 s

Sleep loss slows responses and makes lapses, responses longer than half a second, more frequent.

Lim & Dinges, 2008
14 days

In a lab study, healthy adults aged 21–38 who slept 4 or 6 hours a night for 14 days showed cumulative, dose-dependent deficits in cognitive performance, while self-rated sleepiness barely rose after the first days.

Van Dongen et al., 2003

Caffeine is a common quick fix too; its effects are beyond this guide. For your own tests, the practical point is to compare results taken after similar sleep.

Does warming up or the time of day matter?

Possibly, so keep both constant: researchers treat time of day and practice effects as factors when they measure performance (Blatter & Cajochen, 2007), but no source we checked gives a reaction-time figure for a warm-up.

Compare like with like

Practice effects are a known factor in measuring performance (Blatter & Cajochen, 2007), so compare tries made under similar conditions.

Same time of day

Test at the same hour each time: time of day has its own effect on cognitive performance, apart from how long you have been awake (Blatter & Cajochen, 2007).

Rest between rounds

Long sessions slow you down: average times rose from 228.1 ms in the first block of 20 trials to 237.2 ms in the last (Woods et al., 2015).

FAQ

How long does it take to improve reaction time?

No study we checked gives one number. In a six-week training study, young hockey players improved on the trained choice reaction time task (Schwab & Memmert, 2012); how fast you improve depends on the task and the person. Compare weekly averages on the same device rather than single tries.

Do reaction time games actually work?

The evidence is mixed. Action video game players responded 11% faster than non-players on fast choice tasks with the same accuracy (Dye et al., 2009), but a training study found similar practice effects in action gamers, cognitive gamers and non-gamers on a simple perceptual task (van Ravenzwaaij et al., 2014). No game can promise a specific gain.

Does age stop you getting faster?

Barely. Between 18 and 65 the average rises by about 0.5 ms per year, roughly 21 ms across the whole range (Woods et al., 2015).

Does exercise improve reaction time?

In a study of 120 first-year medical students, those who exercised regularly had faster times than sedentary ones (Jain et al., 2015). That is a comparison between groups: it does not show that exercise made them faster. For how fast athletes react in each sport, see reaction time in sports.

What is the fastest way to react quicker today?

Be rested and alert, test under similar conditions each time, and avoid long sessions without breaks: sleep loss and long sessions both slow responses (Lim & Dinges, 2008) (Woods et al., 2015).
