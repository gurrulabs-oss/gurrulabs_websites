Time estimation test: guess the gap

A time estimation test asks you to guess how long an interval lasted. Two signals, a flash and a beep, mark a hidden gap between 0.30 and 10 s; you guess the seconds and pass within ±0.10 s.

Why do we misjudge short and long times?

Short answer
Because of the “central tendency” effect: people tend to overestimate short intervals and underestimate long ones (Lewis & Miall, 2009). Guesses are pulled toward the middle of the times you have judged.

Timing research covers intervals from a few milliseconds to several seconds (Grondin, 2010), and the size of the bias depends on the task and the person.

How accurate is human time estimation?

It depends on the interval and the task. In Lewis & Miall’s experiments, trained participants’ largest average errors were an overestimate of 44% on short intervals and an underestimate of 19% on long ones, across intervals from 215 ms to 3.59 min (Lewis & Miall, 2009). These are the largest errors in that range, not typical errors for 0.30 to 10 s.

FAQ

How accurate is human time estimation?

It depends on the interval and task. In Lewis & Miall (2009) trained participants’ largest average errors in two production experiments were an overestimate of 44% on short intervals and an underestimate of 19% on long ones (intervals from 215 ms to 3.59 min) (Lewis & Miall, 2009).

Why do short gaps feel longer and long ones shorter?

It is the “central tendency” effect: people tend to overestimate short intervals and underestimate long ones (Lewis & Miall, 2009).

Does the error grow with longer intervals?

Not in a simple way: Haigh et al. (2021) found Weber’s law described sensitivity to duration changes best for 100-3200 ms (Haigh et al., 2021), and Lewis & Miall (2009) found relative variability falling slowly with length (Lewis & Miall, 2009).

Is this a clinical test?

No. It is a playful check of your sense of time, not a diagnostic tool.
