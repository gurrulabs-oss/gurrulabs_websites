Does caffeine improve reaction time?

In studies, usually yes: low to moderate doses went with faster reactions, and one dose restored speed after sleep loss.

On a normal day

A review links low to moderate doses with better alertness, vigilance, attention and reaction time (McLellan et al., 2016). In regular consumers, caffeine also sped simple and choice reaction times (Rogers et al., 2013).

McLellan et al., 2016
After sleep loss

After 44 hours awake, the study gave 600 mg once: speed on a vigilance test was restored and lapses, responses over half a second (Lim & Dinges, 2008), were reduced versus placebo.

Killgore et al., 2008

Most studies report group averages. They show the direction of the effect, not how many milliseconds you personally will gain. The same review found less consistent effects on memory and decision making (McLellan et al., 2016).

How much caffeine did studies use, and how fast does it work?

Reviews cover low to moderate doses, about 40 to 300 mg, and one sleep-loss study gave 600 mg. The effects may begin 15 to 30 minutes after intake and last hours (McLellan et al., 2016) (EFSA, 2015).

Does coffee still work if you drink it every day?

Researchers disagree on how much: some of the gain may simply be relief from overnight withdrawal.

After a night without coffee

In regular consumers, overnight abstinence slowed simple and choice reaction times, and caffeine improved those measures in medium-high consumers (Rogers et al., 2013).

Over the long term

One review found little evidence of net benefit in long-term use versus abstinence, as tolerance develops (James & Rogers, 2005); another concluded that the effects cannot be attributed to withdrawal reversal (Einöther & Giesbrecht, 2013).

The two reviews disagree, so treat withdrawal as an open question, not a settled fact.

Coffee or energy drinks: what are the limits?

The limits are sleep, differences between people and the dose; in one study of energy-drink ingredients, caffeine rather than taurine or glucose was the likely active one (Giles et al., 2012).

Sleep cost

EFSA: a single dose of 100 mg may affect sleep duration and patterns in some adults, especially close to bedtime (EFSA, 2015). Sleep loss itself slows responses (Lim & Dinges, 2008).

It varies

Half-life ranges from two to eight hours in healthy adults (EFSA, 2015), so the same cup can act differently on you and on a friend.

Energy drinks

In one study, caffeine, not taurine or glucose, was the likely cause of the changes in thinking and reaction time (Giles et al., 2012).

Safety note. EFSA found no safety concern for single doses up to 200 mg, or for up to 400 mg a day spread over the day, in healthy adults other than pregnant women; for pregnancy, up to 200 mg a day spread over the day is of no concern for the foetus; for children and adolescents it proposed 3 mg per kg of body weight a day (EFSA, 2015). Its assessment did not cover people with a medical condition or taking medicines: if that is you, ask your doctor. This is not a recommendation to use caffeine.

FAQ

Does coffee improve your reflexes?

In studies, low to moderate doses of caffeine went with better alertness, vigilance and reaction time (McLellan et al., 2016), and a review of double-blind studies found clear benefits for attention (Einöther & Giesbrecht, 2013). The effect varies between people.

How long does caffeine take to work?

EFSA says the effects may begin 15 to 30 minutes after intake and last a number of hours. The average half-life in healthy adults is about four hours, with a range of two to eight (EFSA, 2015).

Do energy drinks improve reaction time?

In one study of habitual consumers after 24 hours without caffeine, caffeine reduced simple and choice reaction time, and the authors judged caffeine, not taurine or glucose, the likely cause of the cognitive changes after energy drinks (Giles et al., 2012). Effects vary and are no promise.

Does caffeine work if you drink coffee every day?

Partly, and researchers disagree. One review found little evidence of net benefit in long-term use versus abstinence, with tolerance to the acute effects (James & Rogers, 2005); another concluded that the effects cannot be attributed to withdrawal reversal (Einöther & Giesbrecht, 2013).
